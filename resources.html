What is a quire?

A quire, in bookmaking, refers to a sheet of paper (or parchment) that has been folded some number of times, creating multiple leaves and pages. The resulting unit, also called a gathering (the terms are usually but not always interchangeable; while all quires are gatherings, not all gatherings are quires), can be nested in or combined with others which, bound together, make a book.

Each fold of the original sheet doubles the number of leaves: fold a sheet once and you get a folio (two leaves, four pages); fold it again to produce a quarto (4º: four leaves, eight pages); again for an octavo (8º), and keep going for sextodecimo (16º), tricesimo-secundo (32º), and sexagesimo-quarto (64º – good luck folding it!). Other intermediate sizes, though not available in QuireMaker, include duodecimo (12º), octodecimo (18º), vicesimo-quarto (24º), and quadragesimo-octavo (48º).

Because, in medieval and early modern printing, a whole sheet is first printed flat on both sides and only folded afterwards, the individual pages are not laid out in reading order on the sheet. Working out which page goes where — so everything reads correctly once folded and trimmed — is called imposition. When multiple quires are bound together or otherwise assembled, the result is a codex: a book, bound on one edge while openable on another, of the kind we still read today. QuireMaker lets you watch that process happen using your own text.
